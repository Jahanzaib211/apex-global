## The decision we've made

Apex is being built as a **global SaaS and technology company** — not a local IT services shop, and
not a call-centre or outsourcing business.

That distinction is the single most important thing in this document, because it changes who we sell
to, what we charge, and what the company is eventually worth.

**A services company sells hours.** It earns money only while people are working. Growth means hiring
more people, and the business is worth a small multiple of its annual profit.

**A SaaS company sells a product.** It earns money whether or not anyone is at a desk that day.
Growth means more customers on software that already exists, and the business is worth many times its
annual revenue.

Apex will do both — technology services fund us and prove our capability, while SaaS products build
the long-term value. But the *identity* is technology and SaaS, and everything on the website will
say so.

**One consequence you should know about:** the call-centre and BPO side is coming off the website
entirely. If that work is still bringing in revenue, we are not stopping it — but it will not be part
of how Apex presents itself, because a buyer who sees "call centre" stops reading before they get to
"software company". Related: our LinkedIn page still advertises BPO, call centre and dispatch. That
needs updating before we run any advertising, or the two will contradict each other.

## Where we stand today

Apex has a LinkedIn page with around 2,000 followers, 11–50 staff, and no website at all.

That last part matters more than it sounds. **We cannot run paid advertising without a website.** Not
"it would help" — we literally cannot. Facebook and Google ads have to send the person somewhere.
Without a destination there is no advertising, and without advertising every client has to come from
someone personally knowing someone. That ceiling is what we're removing.

## What we are building

A premium website that presents Apex as a serious international technology company and converts
visitors into enquiries — plus the private system behind it that manages those enquiries, runs the
website's content, and eventually runs the operations of the business.

Think of it as **the shopfront** and **the back office**.

---

## The shopfront: the public website

### It has to look like a company worth paying

We sell technology. If our own website is slow or looks cheap, the sales conversation is over before
it starts. This one is built to a high standard on our own brand colours — navy, grey, yellow and
off-white from our logo — with a piece of moving 3D graphics on the front page built from our own
logo shape.

That's deliberate. It makes people stop scrolling, and it signals that we can actually build things.

### It has to load fast, especially on phones

Most people who click an ad are on a phone, often on a weak connection. Every extra second of loading
loses roughly a fifth of the people who clicked — people we already paid for.

So the graphics load *after* the page is already usable, and switch off entirely on weak phones.
**If a visual effect ever slows the page down, the effect gets removed.** That rule is not
negotiable, and it's checked automatically every time we change the site.

### What's on it

The headline is:

> **Global Technology. Intelligent Solutions. Built to Scale.**

Then: our SaaS products, nine technology service areas (software development, AI and automation,
cloud, cybersecurity, managed IT, DevOps, digital transformation, technology consulting, and SaaS
development itself), the industries we serve, case studies, a leadership section, articles, careers,
and contact.

Plus **separate mini-pages built specially for advertising**. If we run a campaign for cloud
services, that ad goes to a page about cloud services and nothing else — no menu, no distractions,
one clear action. A focused page like this typically converts two to three times better than sending
people to the homepage.

## The page that wins the big contracts

There's one page worth explaining on its own: a **Technology & Engineering** page showing, with real
diagrams and real numbers, the systems we run and how they're built.

Here's why it matters. A small client believes nice photographs. A serious client — the kind who
signs a large, long contract — sends a technical person to check whether we know what we're talking
about. That person is not impressed by stock imagery. They want to see architecture, monitoring,
security, and how we handle backups and failures.

We can show all of that honestly, because we already run it.

## What we are deliberately *not* claiming

I want to be direct about this, because it protects us.

Sitting on the same machines are things that are **not ours to claim** — an open-source financial
terminal we only downloaded, and a set of security tools other people wrote. We deploy, configure and
run them, and we'll say exactly that. We will not present them as our own work.

The reason is simple: the technical person a client sends to check us out can verify this in about
five minutes, and getting caught inflating a portfolio costs far more than the contract it wins.

This isn't just a promise — it's built into the system. Every item on the website has to be marked as
either *built by Apex*, *operated by Apex*, or *third-party software we run*, and the website
refuses to publish anything that isn't labelled. Client names can't appear without recorded written
permission. Statistics need a traceable source. Phrases like "100% secure" are blocked outright.

We're also keeping trading experiments and dispatch/transport work off the site entirely. They invite
the wrong questions and dilute what Apex is.

## The back office: private, staff only

A password-protected area only our team can reach.

**It holds every enquiry.** Every form, call and WhatsApp tap lands in one list — who they are, what
they want, which country they're in, and **which advert they came from**. That last part is what lets
us say at month-end: we spent this much on this campaign, it produced this many enquiries, this many
became customers. That's the difference between advertising and guessing.

**It lets us run the website ourselves.** Add a service, publish a case study, launch a new SaaS
product page, post a job, write an article — all by clicking around a normal interface. No developer,
no waiting, no cost per change. A website nobody can update goes stale within three months.

**And it has an AI section** that summarises incoming enquiries so a salesperson knows in five
seconds what someone wants, scores which ones look serious, and drafts first versions of copy. You'll
paste in the AI account key through a settings screen — which means we can switch or upgrade
providers later without rebuilding anything. Anything the AI writes is saved as a draft for a human
to approve. Nothing it produces goes live on its own.

## How it grows

The system is built out of **modules** — self-contained blocks, each covering one part of the
business. Like rooms in a building where the foundation, wiring and plumbing are already in place.
Adding a room later means building that room. It does not mean digging up the foundation.

Every module is written up in plain language before it's built, so no knowledge lives only in one
person's head.

Built now: the website, the content system, enquiry tracking, and the AI layer.

Built after, in order: **invoicing** (quotes, invoices, payments), **support** (tickets and response
targets), **customer accounts** (clients log in to see their own invoices, tickets and project
status), **project delivery** (tasks, timesheets, sign-offs), **HR** (staff, attendance, payroll
export), and **marketing** (campaigns and ad spend tracked against actual results).

Most importantly: the structure is designed so that **launching a new SaaS product doesn't require
rebuilding the website**. That's the whole point. If Apex launches five products over three years,
each one is a new entry, not a new project.

## What it costs to run

Modest, and deliberately so.

Everything is built on **free, open-source software** — no licence fees, no per-user charges that
grow as we hire, no vendor who can raise prices or lock away our data. We own the code, the data and
the customer list.

The one place we are choosing to spend money is hosting. The live website will run on a **rented
professional server** — roughly $20–40 a month — rather than on our own hardware at the office.

That's a deliberate decision, and worth understanding. Our own machines are excellent and we'll keep
using them for testing. But a global technology company cannot have its sales website go down because
of a power cut or an internet outage at one building. If that happens in the middle of an advertising
campaign, we're paying for clicks that land on nothing. Forty dollars a month is cheap insurance
against that.

So: rented server for the live site, our own hardware for testing and internal systems. Plus the
domain name and whatever we spend on AI usage.

Compare that to off-the-shelf business software, where a CRM alone runs $25–150 per user per month —
at 50 staff, a five-figure annual bill, forever.
